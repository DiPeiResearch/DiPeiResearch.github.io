# Communication as a mechanism of public health impact

My research develops and evaluates theory-based behavioral interventions and communication strategies with public health impact while examining the cognitive, emotional, and social factors that shape health decisions. Most of my current work focuses on tobacco control and tobacco regulatory science, while earlier research examined antibiotic use, HPV vaccination, and climate and health communication. Across these topics, I ask which mechanisms shape decisions, which strategies reduce misperceptions and support behavior change, and whether effects differ for populations experiencing greater health and social burdens.

<div class="callout"><strong>Unifying premise:</strong> Communication is not a layer added after public health intervention design. It is part of the mechanism through which interventions and policies achieve impact.</div>

## Current research

At Georgia State University, I contribute to NIH-funded studies of tobacco use, cessation, and regulatory policy. A central project is NICER (R01CA239308), a multisite randomized controlled trial evaluating whether theory-based messages can reduce nicotine-related misperceptions and support quitting among adults using very low nicotine cigarettes, including people with low socioeconomic status and those experiencing serious psychological distress. I was lead author of the published study protocol. I also contribute to the design and protocol development of DoubleTake (R01CA289552), a multisite digital cessation study for adults who smoke menthol cigarettes. Across these projects, my work spans formative research, message and measure development, protocol implementation, student training, data management, analysis, and dissemination.

I have also developed a foundation for independent research funding. As principal investigator of a CDC Foundation-funded study, I led a multi-wave randomized experiment with 1,541 young adults in China comparing five anti-smoking video themes and examining cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses over time. I coordinated collaborators across countries, developed and adapted study materials, oversaw data collection, mentored student researchers, and led the preparation of manuscripts and conference presentations. Together, these experiences have prepared me to develop an independent, collaborative, and externally funded research program.

## Methodological strategy

I use both quantitative and qualitative methods because strong communication evidence requires both effect estimation and explanation of how and why effects occur. In 2026, I was selected for the NIH-funded ORBIT Institute at Florida State University College of Medicine, where I received additional training in the systematic development and optimization of behavioral interventions. 

| Method | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Randomized experiments | Message effects and causal contrasts |
| Surveys | Exposure, beliefs, and population heterogeneity |
| Eye-tracking | Visual attention and message processing |
| Focus groups and interviews | Co-creation, interpretation, decision processes, and context |

## Future research directions

My future research asks how tobacco communication can be designed, tailored, and situated within policy environments to support informed decisions, cessation, and health equity while minimizing unintended effects. I will pursue three connected directions.

### 1. Mental health and tobacco communication

I will examine how tobacco messages can address the relationship between smoking and mental health among people experiencing serious psychological distress and other mental health challenges. Building on my current research, I will develop and test messages that address beliefs about smoking as a way to cope with distress, communicate its mental health consequences, and strengthen confidence and a sense of control. Randomized experiments will compare approaches focused on mental health effects, industry manipulation, and psychological empowerment while assessing quit motivation and cessation-related behavior, as well as stigma, distress, reactance, and differences in effects across populations.

### 2. AI and computational message science

I plan to study how generative AI can support the development and delivery of tobacco cessation interventions without replacing theory, community expertise, or randomized evidence. Formative research, iterative experiments, and optimization studies will test when AI-assisted content is acceptable, credible, relevant, and engaging and when it produces uncertainty, mistrust, or resistance. A staged, human-centered pipeline will then evaluate promising strategies in clinical and community settings, particularly for people experiencing psychological distress and socioeconomic disadvantage.
