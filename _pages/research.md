# Communication as a mechanism of public health impact

My research develops and evaluates theory-based behavioral interventions and communication strategies with public health impact while examining the cognitive, emotional, and social factors that shape health decisions. Most of my current work focuses on tobacco control and tobacco regulatory science, while earlier research examined antibiotic use, HPV vaccination, and climate and health communication. Across these topics, I ask which mechanisms shape decisions, which strategies reduce misperceptions and support behavior change, and whether effects differ for populations experiencing greater health and social burdens.

<div class="callout"><strong>Unifying premise:</strong> Communication is not a layer added after public health intervention design. It is part of the mechanism through which interventions and policies achieve impact.</div>

## Current research platforms

### Regulatory communication for very low nicotine cigarettes

Clinical studies can isolate the behavioral effects of reduced-nicotine cigarettes, but policy implementation occurs in a public information environment. This project examines how communication interacts with product use, whether messages can reduce misperceptions, and how responses differ across people with serious psychological distress, people with low socioeconomic status, and a general comparison group.

### Digital interventions for menthol smoking cessation and flavored tobacco communication

This work develops and evaluates digital cessation content for adults who smoke menthol cigarettes and communication about flavored combusted tobacco products, with specific attention to people with low socioeconomic status. Current protocol development combines message co-creation, a discrete choice experiment, eye-tracking, and a waitlist-controlled randomized trial to identify themes and design features that attract attention, shape preferences and sharing intentions, and influence short-term quitting behavior.

### Anti-smoking messages among young adults in China

I lead a CDC Foundation-funded, multi-wave randomized experiment with more than 1,500 young adults in China. The study compares anti-smoking video themes and examines cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses over time, including whether serious psychological distress shapes those responses. A complementary content analysis of open-ended responses examines how participants process themes including tobacco industry manipulation and the mental health consequences of smoking.

## Methodological strategy

I use both quantitative and qualitative methods because strong communication evidence requires both effect estimation and explanation of how and why effects occur. My 2026 NIH ORBIT Institute training further strengthened my approach to the systematic development and optimization of behavioral interventions.

| Method | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Randomized experiments | Message effects and causal contrasts |
| Surveys | Exposure, beliefs, and population heterogeneity |
| Eye-tracking | Visual attention and message processing |
| Focus groups and interviews | Co-creation, interpretation, decision processes, and context |
| Statistical modeling | Mediation, moderation, and multivariable analysis |

## Future research directions

### 1. Mental health and tobacco communication

I plan to develop and test theory-driven messages for people with mental health conditions, including messages about the mental-health consequences of smoking, industry manipulation, and psychological empowerment. Studies will measure persuasive benefits, unintended reactions, and heterogeneous effects rather than assume that one strategy works equally well across populations.

### 2. AI and computational message science

I plan to study how generative AI can support the development and delivery of tobacco cessation interventions without replacing theory, community expertise, or randomized evidence. Formative research, iterative experiments, and optimization studies will test when AI-assisted content is acceptable, credible, relevant, and engaging and when it produces uncertainty, mistrust, or resistance. A staged, human-centered pipeline will then evaluate promising strategies in clinical and community settings, particularly for people experiencing psychological distress and socioeconomic disadvantage.

<ol class="ai-pipeline" aria-label="Proposed AI research pipeline">
  <li><strong>Ground and generate</strong><span>Compare retrieval-augmented large language models with parameter-efficient adaptation such as LoRA for theory-constrained message generation. Use curated evidence and explicit communication objectives to limit unsupported claims.</span></li>
  <li><strong>Represent multimodal messages</strong><span>Use multimodal neural networks, including vision-language encoders and cross-attention transformers, to model how text, imagery, audience characteristics, and experimental responses interact.</span></li>
  <li><strong>Model processing over time</strong><span>Evaluate temporal transformers and state-space models for eye-tracking scanpaths, repeated exposure, and longitudinal message-response sequences.</span></li>
  <li><strong>Model relationships and effects</strong><span>Test graph neural networks for message diffusion and audience-message relationships, alongside causal representation learning and treatment-effect models for heterogeneous message effects.</span></li>
  <li><strong>Adapt only after validation</strong><span>Consider contextual bandits and offline reinforcement learning for adaptive delivery only after preregistered experiments establish safety, efficacy, and equity across priority populations.</span></li>
</ol>

Across these studies, candidate messages will undergo automated screening, expert and community review, cultural and accessibility review, and behavioral testing. Models will be compared on theoretical fidelity, factual support, subgroup performance, transparency, and experimentally measured outcomes.

### 3. Modified-risk claims and tobacco-product use transitions

I will study how modified-risk claims shape risk understanding and longitudinal product use transitions. This work will distinguish temporary substitution from stable dual use and complete switching while assessing cigarette consumption, quit attempts, delayed cessation, and unintended uptake over time.
